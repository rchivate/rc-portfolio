Findings: What Factors Influenced Survival on the Titanic?

Posted on April 5th, 2026 by Riya C

The Social Reality of the Titanic

On April 15, 1912, the Titanic sank and more than 67% of the people on board lost their lives. While we often think of this as a random tragedy caused by an iceberg, the data shows that survival was actually tied to social status. It was not just luck. Survival was shaped by the "women and children first" rule and the physical layout of the ship, where first-class luxury was much closer to the lifeboats than the cramped third-class sections.

What the Data Tells Us

When we looked at the passenger list, we found that certain details acted like a roadmap for who would survive:

  • Class and Title: A person's social standing was the biggest factor. Having a title like "Miss" or being in a first-class cabin made a massive difference in staying alive.
  • Family Connections: Being alone was not always an advantage. People in small families or pairs tended to do better than those traveling completely by themselves or in very large groups that could get separated in a crisis.
  • Gender: The historical accounts are true. Women had a much higher survival rate than men across almost every category.

Testing the Models

We used three different computer models to see which one could best predict these outcomes based on the facts we had.

  • Logistic Regression: This one was okay at seeing the general trends but it missed a lot of the actual survivors in the data.
  • Random Forest: This model was very careful. When it said someone survived, it was usually right, but it was too cautious and missed too many people.
  • Decision Tree: This was our most effective tool. It was the most balanced at identifying survivors correctly without making too many mistakes.

Conclusion

By looking at 891 individual stories, we can see that the sinking of the Titanic was not just a maritime accident. It was a reflection of the world at that time. Our best model shows that even in a disaster, the social structures of the era were the most powerful forces at play

Social Determinants of Health and the Capabilities Approach

Posted on February 20th, 2026 by Riya C

Social Determinants of Health are conditions that affect the health and the well-being of the individuals living in the area. But these conditions are non medical. Some examples of these determinants are distance to the local hospital or grocery store, the quality of education, etc. All of these follow the common theme of how these utilities or the lack thereof affect a person's life. For example, if the education quality in the area is not good, then they will have trouble getting a job. Struggling to get a job will lead to financial crisis. And all of that will circle back to not being able to afford certain commodities such as food, healthcare, etc.

The Capabilities approach is essentially looking at what a perosn is capable of doing in an environemnt. For example if we take the example of education, what is the highest amount of education that the person can attain. Along with that, what are the barriers that they might encounter while attempting to pursue the desired education. Are there any roadblocks. Another good example is food. A person can choose to not eat, or they can be forced to not eat due to their circumstances. A person may be forced to not eat because of their health or financial status. But, a person may choose not to eat during a fast that they are partaking in willingly. This distinction can help clear out many assumptions.

Both of these variables work together. By understanding the environment in which a person is raised in helps to understand how their life is going to play out in terms of the opportunities that they are able to explore. These factors also determine whether a person is capable or not of performing a certain activity. By understanding the capabilities, we have a base udnerstanding and background information on the person that clears out any assumptions. A good example of both of these working together can be explained through education- the example that I have been using to explain the terms separately. Firstly, we must find out what are the different factors that are allowing/preventing education. We can take for instance the lack of public transportation in an area. If there is a lack of public transport, it is harder to get to school for some students especially if they do not have a car or a bike. In this case, a person is capable of going to school- they would walk- but they are not able to because of their circumstances. Because they were not able to get quality education, they may be struggling financially which can lead them to not get enough money. Thus, they are not able to afford good food. and may be forced to fast. By understanding the root issue and then analyzing further from there helps us see the bigger picture and eliminates assumptions.

Ethics and Data

Posted on January 27th, 2026 by Riya C

A data modeling practice can be ethically ambigious instead of straight black and white ethical or unethical. The problem that Data Scientists are trying to solve may be a legitimate problem that needs to be studied or figured out, but the extraction of data or the manipulation of data surrounding the problem may be unethical. One case that I can think of is if a Data Scientist wants to study the effects of a certain drug on patients with a specific disease. The problem is legitimate because the drug needs to be tested before it goes into the market. But the data extraction process is not the most ideal seeing that the drug would need to be tested on people. These people are not even regular, healthy individuals. This literally makes it a life or death scenario. Even though the intention may be there, the execution would not be ethical if they decided to test on live patients.

I will list three practices that I think are beneficial to ensure ethical data modeling: Transparency, Data Auditing, and Stakeholder Engagement.

1. Transparency: Being open about the data sources, methods, and potential biases in the modeling process. This helps build trust and allows for external scrutiny. Transparency also reduces any surprises that the participants may encounter. They are well aware of all the aspects of the data collection/experiment/methodology. Communication is key in these types of scenario.

2. Data Auditing: Regularly reviewing and auditing data collection and processing methods to ensure they comply with ethical standards and regulations. This redices any biases that the data may have. This includes historical biases, gender bias, etc. This is to ensure that the data doesn't favor any group, individual, or an experience.

3. Stakeholder Engagement: Involving stakeholders, including those who may be affected by the data modeling outcomes, in the decision-making process to ensure their perspectives and concerns are considered. This helps because the data scientist gets an outside perspective. when you are scrutinizing your own work, you do not find flaws. But when there is a third person who is looking at it from a clean slate perspective, they will be able to honestly tell you if there are any problems with the methodology.

Context is important in Data Science

Posted on January 16th, 2026 by Riya C

Context matters heavily in Data Science. Data can be manipulated in many different ways which sometimes lead to misleading results. For example, the classic example of the relationship between Ice Cream sales and Shark Attacks at the beach. The raw data proved that an increase in Ice Cream sales can lead to higher rates of Shark Attacks. If you look closesly at these two variables, there is no research or evidence that is backing this statement. Just because there is correlation between two variables does not mean that there is causation in any way. This is a prime example of when context matters. The data was collected during the season of Summer. Obviously, more people will go to the beach during the summer, and most likely buy ice cream. Because there are more people at the beach, there will be more recorded shark attacks because there are people for the sharks to attack.

Modeling social topics and people are important in Data Science because they provide the necessary background and understanding to interpret data accurately and ethically. By keeping in mind the context of the data, we can ensure that our models are not only accurate but also fair and responsible. With great power comes great responsibility. Using data that are about real people helps us understand their situation in the context of the dataset. There are some measures that data scientists much ensure that they are taking to make sure that the people whose data is being used are not being harmed. For example, if there is a dataset with patients' data, the name and other sensitive data should not be available publicly. This is an invasion of privacy.

I am hoping to better understand how to ensure that the data is used ethically and some steps that are necessary to take before manipulating the data. I also want to learn how to manipulate data so that it best represents the situation and the context in which the data was taken from.
